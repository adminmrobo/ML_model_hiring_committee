Linear regression — the line with the smallest squared error

From today, the question changes. Not "Is it over 35 MPa?" but "How many MPa is this mix?" The answer is a number with size, not a label, so this is regression. The Regression Team's hiring bar is the field formula Mr. Manual brought. It is an old formula that calculates strength from just the water–binder ratio and the age.

Linear regression predicts strength with one value: each feature multiplied by a weight and added up. It picks the weights that make the sum of the squared differences between prediction and reality (residuals) smallest. This is least squares. The answer comes out of one matrix calculation (the normal equation), and gradient descent, stepping down little by little, reaches the same place.

2. Linear model Linear Model

Multiply features by weights, add them up, and add the intercept: that is the prediction.

ŷ = w1x1 + w2x2 + … + wnxn + b
→ 01 Self-introduction

3. Residual Residual

The difference between the actual and predicted values. The vertical distance from a point to the line.

ei = yi − ŷi  (+ under-predicted, − over-predicted)
→ 01 Self-introduction

4. Least squares Least Squares

Choose the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals.

minimize  MSE = 1n Σ (yi − ŷi)2
→ 01 Self-introduction

5. Normal equation Normal Equation

Solve for where the gradient is 0, and the weights come out in one go.

β = (XTX)−1 XTy
X: n mix rows × (1 + features)  ·  y: actual strength
→ 04 Probation

6. Gradient descent Gradient Descent

Move the weights little by little in the direction that reduces the loss, and reach the same bottom.

w ← w − η · ∂ MSE∂w
→ 04 Probation

7. Metrics MAE · RMSE · R²

How many MPa off on average, how big the big mistakes are, and how much better than the mean line.

MAE = mean |y − ŷ|
RMSE = √ mean (y − ŷ)2
R2 = 1 − Σ (y − ŷ)2Σ (y − ȳ)2

For standardized features, wj = change in ŷ (MPa) when that feature grows by 1 std. dev.

01
Self-introduction · Concept

Self-introduction — the line with the smallest squared residuals

Line ŷ = w·x + b → residual e = y − ŷ → square and average (MSE) → the smallest w, b

β
Candidate · Linear regressionI draw one straight line through the points. I square how far the line misses each point, add them all up, and choose the line with the smallest total. Put a new mix into the chosen line, and its height is my answer.
Try it · a line that predicts strength from one feature (772 training mixes)

Why is the first line flat?

Slope 0, and the height is the mean strength of the training mixes. It is a line that answers only "35.8 MPa on average" to any mix, and this is the regression baseline. R² measures by what percentage the sum of squared residuals is reduced compared with this mean line. So the R² of the mean line is exactly 0. Move the slope and look for the direction where R² becomes greater than 0.

R² = 1 − (my line's sum of squared residuals) ÷ (mean line's sum of squared residuals)

Why squares, of all things?

If you just add up the residuals, + and − cancel, and even a nonsense line can sum to 0. You could add absolute values (that gives a different regression that minimizes MAE), but squaring makes the derivative clean, so the answer comes out as one formula (the normal equation in Section 04). In exchange, it punishes big residuals more, so the line gets pulled by a few outliers. In "Show squared residuals," the big squares are exactly that pulling force.

MSE = mean[ (y − ŷ)² ]  ·  RMSE = √MSE (the unit returns to MPa)

What you see when you change the feature

For the water–binder ratio, higher means lower strength, so the slope is negative. For cement it is positive. Pick age and the points draw a curve that rises steeply at first and then levels off, which a straight line cannot follow. Near 7 days and 365 days the line passes above the points, and at 28–90 days it passes below. This mismatch is the "pattern in the residuals" of Section 05, and it decides this interview.

One row is one mix (sample); the mix quantities and age are the features (X); compressive strength (MPa) itself is the target (y)

Task · Regression

Predict compressive strength (MPa)

In 5-4, strength was turned into Pass/Fail labels at the 35 MPa threshold. This interview predicts the strength number as is, with no threshold (box 1 of the application). The 772 training mixes have a mean of 35.8 MPa and a standard deviation of 16.7 MPa. The baseline that always says the mean has a test MAE of 13.52 MPa.

Regression Team hiring bar · the empirical formula of current employee Mr. Manual (no learning)
f₂₈ = 96.5 ÷ 7water–binder ratio  →  f(t) = f₂₈ · t ÷ (4 + 0.85t)

The first part is Abrams' law, over 100 years old: "more water means weaker." The second part is the ACI 209R correction giving the strength at age t days as a multiple of the 28-day strength. The four numbers (96.5, 7, 4, 0.85) are textbook values, not learned from this data. Exam-paper R² 0.735, MAE 6.72 MPa. Put in the water–cement ratio as in the original formula, and R² collapses to 0.058. The knowledge that slag and fly ash also harden is what saves the formula.

Does linear regression need standardization?

Unregularized linear regression gives exactly the same predictions with standardization. If you write cement in tons instead of kg, the weight just grows 1,000× and the line stays the same (check it in "Change the conditions and look again" in the interview result). There are still two reasons to standardize. ① You can compare weight sizes (Section 06). ② Gradient descent converges much faster (Section 04). From the regularization in 5-3 (Ridge·Lasso) on, standardization changes the score itself. The mean and standard deviation come from the training data only.

Swapping the data — to use your own factory's data

  • All you need is a table (CSV, etc.) with one mix (or lot) per row and one column of the number you want to predict (strength, slump, yield, etc.).
  • Change only three places in cell 1 of the Colab code (Section A): the data loading, FEATURES and the target column.
  • Replace the hiring bar with your site's empirical formula or the calculation sheet you use now. If you have none, first compare with the "always the mean" baseline.
  • Text features (plant, raw material grade) are turned into numeric columns with one-hot encoding. Fill in or remove blanks first.
  • Check the unit of the target. MAE and RMSE come out in the same unit as the target. Looking only at R² loses your sense of units.

Getting code from an AI assistant — delegate, but verify

① Prompt → ② Spot the mistakes → ③ Checklist → ④ Check with the run results

Linear regression code is short, so it seems the AI will hardly get it wrong. But the shorter the code, the more one wrong line changes the whole result. If the answer column slips into the features, R² comes out close to 1; if you grade on the training data, it looks better than it really is. The committee's rule is the same: code from an AI is like a candidate's cover letter. Read it, doubt it, run it and check.

    The normal equation — the bottom is where the gradient is 0
    
      
    MSE is a quadratic in the weights, so it is bowl-shaped with a single bottom. At the bottom the gradient is 0, and writing that condition as equations gives the system (XᵀX)β = Xᵀy. With 8 features, you solve one 9×9 system and you are done. No iterations, no learning rate.
    
      
    β = (XᵀX)−1 Xᵀy  — with d features, solve a (d+1)×(d+1) matrix
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Gradient descent — so why bother walking down?
    
      
    With tens of thousands of features, XᵀX becomes a tens-of-thousands-square matrix that is hard to solve, and if the data does not fit in memory, you cannot even build XᵀX. Gradient descent walks while looking at a little data at a time, so it is used in such cases. For models like neural networks, whose bottom cannot be solved by formula, gradient descent is the only way. The formula for one step has the same form as 5-4 logistic regression: the average of "how wrong × feature."
    
      
    w ← w + η · 2·mean[ (y − ŷ) · x ]  ·  b ← b + η · 2·mean[ y − ŷ ]
    
    
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Budget officer · Money-RoboOne 9×9 system and probation's over? If a thousand steps of gradient descent end up in the same spot, at our plant's scale, solving it in one go is cheaper.

    How to read R²
    
      
    R² 0.567 means "it reduced the sum of squared residuals of just saying the mean by 56.7%." You can also read it as explaining a little over half the spread in strength. R² has no unit, which makes it good for comparing with other data, but it does not tell the field "how many MPa off." So always use it with MAE. On the exam paper R² can even be negative, meaning worse than the mean (the degree-4 polynomial in 5-3).

    HR card — where to deploy it and how to manage it
    
    
    Strengths and weaknesses, good and poor fits, working conditions (hyperparameters)
    
  
    
  
    
    
    
      
    βAPP-02 → EMP-0002Linear regressionSupervised Division › Regression Team
    
      
    In one line: a straight line that does good work when given good features. Its training is the fastest, and it can explain itself with weights. It cannot draw curves on its own, but when a person straightens the features, it beats the empirical formula.
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Strengths
    
        
      
          
    • Training finishes in one go. One normal equation, no iterations or learning rate (Section 04).
      • 
          
    • It can explain itself. A weight is exactly "how many MPa strength changes when that feature grows" (chart below).
      • 
          
    • Lightweight. Numbers to memorize: just the number of features + 1. It can even predict in one line of Excel.
      • 
          
    • A good baseline. The first candidate to run on a regression problem. Other candidates need to beat this score to be worth using.
      • 
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Weaknesses
    
        
      
          
    • It can't draw curves. Bending relationships like strength with age need a person to make features such as log (Section 05).
      • 
          
    • It doesn't know interactions. Effects where two features work together, like "slag only shows its strength with long curing," need multiplied features (5-3).
      • 
          
    • It gets pulled by outliers. With squared loss, a few big residuals pull the line.
      • 
          
    • With features that resemble each other (mix quantities whose total is almost constant, like cement, water and aggregate), weights wobble and their signs can even flip.
      •

    Working conditions · hyperparameters often adjusted
    
    
      
    ArgumentDefaultMeaning and usage
    
      
        
    LinearRegression(fit_intercept)TrueWhether to have the intercept b. Almost always True. False forces the line through the origin.
    
        
    LinearRegression(positive)FalseTrue keeps all weights at 0 or above. Use it when you want to block signs that make no sense, like "adding more cement makes it weaker."
    
        
    Feature list—The real working condition of linear regression. It has almost no hyperparameters; instead, what you put in decides the score. log(age) alone takes R² from 0.567 → 0.844 (Section 05).
    
        
    SGDRegressor(eta0, max_iter)0.01, 1000The same candidate under another name, solved by gradient descent. Used when the data is very large. Standardization is essential, and the default is penalty="l2" (regularized), so set penalty=None to match pure linear regression.
    
        
    Ridge(alpha) · Lasso(alpha)1.0Linear regression with a penalty added on the weights. Used when there are many features or features resemble each other. Interviewed in 5-3. It runs in the opposite direction to logistic regression's C (C ≈ 1/alpha).

      Good fit · poor fit
      
    
        
    Good fitNumber prediction where the relationship is mostly straight (raw material → cost, temperature → power use), reports that must explain with weights, tabular data where experts can supply good features (water–binder ratio, log age), a first baseline for other regression models

      Notes on the data, images and concept of this page
      
  
      • Data and calculations. The practical exam is the UCI Concrete Compressive Strength data, 1,030 mixes; the exam paper is the 258 mixes split with test_size=0.25, stratify=3 strength grades, random_state=42. Mr. Manual's empirical formula, the Regression Team hiring bar, is Abrams' law (f28 = 96.5 / 7^water–binder ratio, textbook constants A = 14,000 psi ≈ 96.5 MPa, B = 7) multiplied by the ACI 209R age correction t / (4 + 0.85t); the formula, originally written with the water–cement ratio, was widened to the water–binder ratio and set for teaching. It is not a standard for real mix design or quality judgment. The numbers in Sections 01·04·05·06 and the interview result are calculated directly in the browser by this page (the normal equation is solved by Gaussian elimination, and the probation replay by gradient descent). They match the Colab results (Section A) to the third decimal place. The 'AI-written code' in Section 03 is a deliberately constructed example collecting common mistakes, not an actual answer from any particular AI service.
      • Application, page 1. The 9-panel application in section 00 of each episode was newly drawn for this series. The logistic regression episode follows the layout of a widely used 9-panel summary infographic (binary classification → linear score → sigmoid → probability → boundary → threshold → loss → learning → multiclass classification); the original images and logos were not copied.
      • Images. The character images are reused from the "AI Algorithm Family Org Chart". The interview room scenes, ID badges and charts are drawn directly by this page in code.
      • The "Hiring Committee" concept. It is a metaphor that compares models to candidates and training and evaluation to an interview. It does not represent a real hiring process or any specific company. Not being hired only means "not a fit for this job"; it does not rank the models.
      • Characters. Money-Robo is the mascot of a service run by the author. The Doctor, the Assistant, the fruit fly researcher, Mr. Manual and the candidates do not represent real people. The money and budget talk in the dialogue is a verbal habit standing for computing cost.
      • Data. The practical exam of the Supervised Division is the Concrete Compressive Strength dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository (Yeh, 1998, 1,030 cases, CC BY 4.0). The exam-paper split is the same in every episode: test_size=0.25, stratify=three strength grades, random_state=42 (772 training · 258 test). The strength grades (25 · 45 MPa) and the pass criterion (35 MPa) are thresholds this series set for teaching; they do not replace any design standard.
      • Language. This series is written in Korean. Translations (English · 中文 · 日本語 · Oʻzbekcha · Русский) are added under the same file names after the Korean content is final. Comments and output in the Colab code are left in the original Korean.
